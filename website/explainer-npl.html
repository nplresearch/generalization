Research Explainer · June 2025

The Curse of Generalization

Why the same property that makes neural networks powerful—shared, distributed representations—imposes hard limits on what they can do.

Based on "Bound by Semanticity" (Nurisso et al.) and related work from the Princeton-NPL collaboration

Here's a puzzle that should bother you: state-of-the-art vision-language models can write poetry about an image, identify obscure art styles, and reason about complex scenes—but ask them to count how many apples are on a table, and they fall apart.

GPT-4V struggles with counting. Gemini confuses spatial relationships. DALL-E can't reliably generate "three red cubes." These aren't edge cases or cherry-picked failures. They're systematic.

For years, researchers treated this as an engineering problem—something we'd fix with more data, better architectures, or clever prompting. But a series of recent papers from Princeton and collaborators suggests something more fundamental is going on.

The same property that makes these models so good at generalization—shared, distributed representations—imposes hard limits on their ability to process multiple items at once.

This isn't a bug to be fixed. It's a law to be understood.

Background

A Decade of Convergence

The result we'll discuss—an exact mathematical tradeoff between generalization and identification—is the culmination of several research threads converging over the past decade.

Rationalizing Constraints on the Capacity for Cognitive Control
Musslick & Cohen · Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2021

In 2021, Sebastian Musslick and Jonathan Cohen argued that human multitasking limits aren't arbitrary—they emerge from a fundamental computational tradeoff. Neural systems that share representations across tasks can learn quickly and generalize flexibly, but they pay a cost: those same shared representations cause interference when you try to do multiple things at once.

Learning Representations that Support Extrapolation
Webb, Dulberg, Frankland et al. · ICML, 2020

Meanwhile, Taylor Webb and colleagues were approaching a related problem: why do neural networks struggle to extrapolate beyond their training data? They found that representations supporting extrapolation need to capture relational structure—the relationships between stimuli, not just their absolute features.

No Coincidence, George: Processing Limits as the Curse of Generalization
Frankland, Webb, Lewis & Cohen · PsyArXiv, 2025

The pieces came together in a 2025 preprint that gave the phenomenon a name: the curse of generalization. The "Bound by Semanticity" paper provides the precise formalization.

The Framework

Two Tasks Every Brain Must Do

The paper formalizes the tradeoff using two canonical tasks from cognitive psychology:

  1. Generalization (Similarity): Given two reference stimuli and a probe, which reference is the probe more similar to?
  2. Identification: Given two references and told the probe equals one of them, which one is it?

At first glance, these should go hand-in-hand. A good representation should excel at both, right?

Wrong. There's a deep tension—one that connects to the binding problem in cognitive science.

The Binding Problem

When you see a red square and a blue circle, how does your brain keep track of which color goes with which shape? Distributed representations make this hard—the "red" and "square" features might activate overlapping neurons. Without some mechanism to keep them separate, you get binding errors.

To generalize well, representations need to be distributed—similar things have overlapping, fuzzy representations that capture shared structure. But to identify well, representations need to be sharp—each stimulus needs its own distinct signature.

The Math

Semantic Resolution

The paper's core insight is that the tradeoff is governed by a single parameter: semantic resolution, denoted ε.

Your model has a similarity function g(x, y) that measures how "close" two stimuli are in representation space. The resolution ε is essentially the radius within which your model can meaningfully compare things.

Key Intuition

If ε is small, your similarity function is sharp—you can tell nearby things apart, but lose relationships between distant things. If ε is large, your similarity function is broad—you capture long-range structure, but nearby things blur together.

For a "constant similarity" model (things within ε are similar, things beyond are not), the probabilities of success have closed-form expressions:

$$p_S = \frac{1}{2} + b - b^2 \qquad p_I = 1 - \frac{b}{2}$$

where b = b(ε) is the fraction of the stimulus space covered by a ball of radius ε.

Scaling

The 1/n Collapse

What happens when you have n items instead of 2? The news is bad: identification performance collapses as 1/n:

$$p_I^n \approx \frac{1}{b \cdot n}$$

If your model has learned representations that support generalization (b ≈ 0.5), going from 2 items to 10 items drops identification accuracy by roughly 5×.

Miller's Law Explained

The famous "7 ± 2" limit on working memory items isn't an implementation detail—it's a fundamental consequence of using representations structured for generalization. Any system that can flexibly generalize will hit capacity limits on multi-item processing.

Emergence

Emergence in Neural Networks

The theoretical results above assume a particular form for the similarity function. But what happens when you train a neural network end-to-end?

A minimal 2-layer ReLU network, trained on a similarity task, spontaneously develops a resolution boundary. The network learns to represent nearby stimuli with positive inner products and distant stimuli with negative inner products—which the ReLU then clips to zero.

During training, the empirical (pS, pI) trajectory closely tracks the theoretical Pareto front. The network doesn't know about the equations—it discovers them through gradient descent.

Validation

From Theory to Reality

These limits aren't artifacts of simple setups. The paper tests CNNs on bird species classification, LLMs on temporal comparisons, and VLMs on spatial proximity tasks.

In all cases, models exhibit clear resolution limits. The signature is universal: accuracy is high within the resolution boundary, then drops to chance beyond it.

This explains why VLMs can describe complex scenes but fail at counting: scene description relies on coarse-grained semantic relationships (large ε), while counting requires fine-grained identification (small ε).

Implications

What This Means

1. Architecture Won't Save You

The Pareto front is independent of model architecture. More parameters can improve where you are on the front, but can't push you above it.

2. The Tradeoff Is Rational

Given that representations supporting generalization necessarily cause interference, the observed limits on multitasking and working memory aren't design flaws—they're optimal given the goal of flexible intelligence.

3. VLM Failures Are a Feature

These models have learned compositional, distributed representations (which is why they generalize so impressively), and such representations necessarily produce interference when processing multiple items.

"The binding errors exhibited by VLMs suggest that they have developed compositional representations, perhaps as a consequence of being forced to generalize by their immense and highly diverse training corpora." — Campbell et al., NeurIPS 2024
Open Questions

What's Next

  • Can we measure semantic resolution in pretrained models?
  • How do humans navigate this tradeoff? Does the brain dynamically adjust representation sharpness?
  • Can compositional representations escape the bound?
  • Is there an optimal training curriculum for controlling where a model lands on the Pareto front?

If the curse of generalization is truly fundamental, it suggests that human-level general intelligence may require architectural innovations we haven't yet discovered.
